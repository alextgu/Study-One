Research consistently confirms that "working with an expert personal tutor is generally regarded as 
the most efficient form of education" (Kestin et al., 2025). This is because a tutor can adapt to the 
individual ability and specific needs of a student, whereas traditional university structures—often 
involving one teacher working with hundreds of students—suffer from systemic inefficiencies. These 
passive classroom environments inherently fail to cater to every individual, as the instruction 
often moves "too quickly for some students and too slowly for others because the teacher controls 
the pace" (Kestin et al., 2025). While personal tutoring is ideal, it is practically impossible 
to meet the needs of a diverse and large student body. As AI becomes intertwined with the student 
experience, many are turning to chatbots to fill institutional gaps; however, this shift poses new 
risks. Most commercial AI chatbots are "generally designed to be helpful, not to promote learning". 
This "unguided use" often allows students to complete their assignments without actually engaging in 
critical thinking, essentially using the technology as a shortcut, creating a dangerous cycle of 
over-reliance and inefficient studying which can "diminish human agency and critical thinking for 
both students and educators" (Cotilla Conceição & van der Stappen, 2025). 

The empirical evidence for structured AI tutoring reveals significant advantages in both performance
 and time efficiency. A randomized controlled trial demonstrated that students using a custom-built 
 AI tutor learned significantly more than those in a traditional active learning classroom, with the 
 AI group achieving a median post-test score of 4.5 compared to 3.5 in the control group 
 (Kestin et al., 2025). Furthermore, 70% of students in the AI group reached these milestones in 
 under 60 minutes, with a median time on task of only 49 minutes, and quantile regression analysis 
 indicated a substantial effect size in the range of 0.73 to 1.3 standard deviations 
 (Kestin et al., 2025). This efficiency is largely attributed to the AI's capacity for "personalized 
 feedback on demand" and "self-pacing," which allows students to fill unique conceptual gaps without 
 being "tethered to a synchronous lecture" (Kestin et al., 2025). By using detailed, step-by-step 
 solutions to guide students through sequential problems, structured systems can maintain high 
 engagement while mitigating the "hallucinations" common in unguided large language models.
However, the implementation of AI-assisted learning must navigate "persistent problems" regarding 
inequitable access and ethical governance (Cotilla Conceição & van der Stappen, 2025). Higher 
education systems frequently fail to support "first-generation students," "minorities," and those 
from "low-income backgrounds" due to a lack of inclusive practices and "socioeconomic inequality" 
(Cotilla Conceição & van der Stappen, 2025). While current AI adoptions often focus on "institutional 
efficiency rather than fostering equity," research suggests that "culturally responsive AI tasks" 
can empower marginalized learners by allowing them to leverage lived experiences 
(Cotilla Conceição & van der Stappen, 2025). Ethical concerns also persist regarding the "black box" 
nature of AI algorithms, which creates a "lack of transparency" that makes it difficult to identify 
or correct "algorithmic bias" (Cotilla Conceição & van der Stappen, 2025). To address these challenges, 
researchers advocate for a "flipped classroom" approach where AI is used to bring all students to 
a baseline level of understanding before class, reserving in-person time for "higher-order skills 
such as advanced problem solving" (Kestin et al., 2025; Cotilla Conceição & van der Stappen, 2025). 
This framework ensures AI remains a "tool for students" that supports the development of critical
 thinking rather than replacing human autonomy (Cotilla Conceição & van der Stappen, 2025).
